\begin{abstract}

Probes trained on a language model's hidden states separate true statements from false ones at high accuracy, and this is read as evidence that the model represents truth internally. We show that these probes largely track how common the statement's entity is. On a corpus of 608 statements crossing truth against entity frequency, probes trained the standard way score 0.761 to 0.998 where the two properties agree and average 0.435 where they disagree, with ten of eighteen models below chance. Stripped of every false statement, the same probes sort common entities from rare ones at AUROC 0.766 to 0.961 with truth held constant. A training-free detector that reads no hidden states reaches 0.830 on the same split, and one probe in eighteen beats it. The truth signal is nonetheless recoverable: removing the entity-frequency direction by iterative nullspace projection, using pretraining-corpus counts and no truth labels, raises mean off-diagonal AUROC from 0.409 to 0.768 on every one of the eighteen models, while the benchmark's own score falls from 0.968 to 0.718. We release the corpus, the auditors and the build-time gates.

\end{abstract}
\section{Introduction}

A probe trained on a language model's hidden states separates true statements from false ones and reports high accuracy. This is read as evidence that the model represents truth internally \citep{azaria2023internal}, and the approach has been extended in several directions since \citep{marks2023geometry,burns2023discovering,burger2024truth}. In the datasets these probes are trained on, true statements are also the ordinary ones, so a probe that learned only ``familiar means true'' would report the same number.

We can measure how much of the score that alternative accounts for. Take probes trained the standard way, discard every false statement, and ask them to separate common entities from rare ones among statements that are all true: AUROC \textbf{0.766 to 0.961} across 18 models from 70M to 12B parameters. Truth is held constant, so whatever produces those scores is not truth.

To separate the two properties we crossed them. Each statement is true or false and concerns a common or a rare entity, giving four cells. Standard datasets sit on the true-common and false-rare diagonal fully on the attestedness axis their construction uses, and partially on the entity- frequency axis we measure (§A.6). We train there, exactly as the field does, and test on the other two cells, where they disagree. On the diagonal our probes score 0.761 to 0.998. Off it, the same probes at the same layers average \textbf{0.435} and range from \textbf{0.08 to 0.89}, with ten of the eighteen below chance. The average is below chance, which is the more precise finding: off the diagonal these probes are not failing at random, they are inverted, tracking familiarity against truth.

The truth signal survives underneath the confound and can be separated from it. Removing the entity-frequency direction from the representation by iterative nullspace projection, then retraining the truth probe on the projected representation, raises mean off-diagonal AUROC from \textbf{0.409 to 0.768}, with a gain on \textbf{every one of the 18 models}, averaging \textbf{0.360}. The erasure experiments use a linear probe rather than the feedforward classifier of A.1, and both baseline and erased arms use the same one, so the gain is a within-probe comparison; A.2 defines the estimator. The projection uses frequency labels only and never truth labels, and entity frequency is counted from pretraining corpora, so the labels it needs cost nothing to produce. Projecting out the same number of random directions instead changes nothing at all, 0.408 against a baseline of 0.409, so what is recovered is that specific direction and not the effect of discarding dimensions. How much is recovered tracks model capability: 0.456 on the weakest substrate to 0.986 on the strongest.

Two further results place this. A detector reading no hidden states, perplexity under a reference model, reaches \textbf{0.830} on the same off-diagonal split, and under a paired per-item test exactly \textbf{1 of 18} probes beats it. And the standard benchmark cannot identify which probes are affected: restricted to probes it would itself endorse, \textbf{33 of 64} are still below chance off the diagonal.

Our corpus is built to make the comparison fair rather than to be novel. On the diagonal we deliberately reuse the field's own released items, and 75.3\% of our authored statements appear verbatim in Azaria and Mitchell's files, because training on anything else would test a different distribution from the one the field reports. The two off-diagonal cells, where truth and familiarity disagree, are new; they are where the two come apart. Section 8 and Appendices A.4 and A.11 give the construction, its defects, and the gates that now catch them.

The four cells are the design (Figure~\ref{fig:design}). \textbf{TT} is true and common. \textbf{TA} is true but rare. \textbf{FT} is false but fluent and ordinary sounding. \textbf{FA} is false and odd. Standard benchmarks live almost entirely on the TT/FA diagonal, where truth and typicality agree. The off diagonal is where they disagree, and it is the only place the question can be asked.

Typicality is entity frequency from pretraining-corpus counts, not perplexity. We registered a plan to measure typicality three ways and check that they agree (decisions.md D-002, commit \texttt{e17707f}, 2026-07-09). Two of the three are measurable without circularity, and they do not agree, which settled the choice for us; the promotion of the frequency axis from cross-check to primary is recorded in D-007.

Frequency and reference perplexity are close to unrelated on this corpus: Spearman 0.058, p = 0.16. They also load on different things. Standardizing by the total standard deviation (Appendix A.2), frequency separates typical from atypical items by 1.082 and separates true from false by 0.032, so it tracks familiarity and carries almost no truth signal. Perplexity does the reverse, separating true from false by 1.171 and typical from atypical by 0.036. Median reference perplexity is 17.2 for common true statements and 85.0 for odd false ones, while within a truth class it barely moves. A corpus built on the perplexity axis would have sorted mostly by truth and left the cell this study depends on empty. The third measure we planned, the model's own probability for the claim, is excluded as circular: it is read from the same network the probe reads.

The axes are crossed within each domain. Median log frequency by cell: cities 6.65 / 5.40 / 6.71 / 5.33 for TT / TA / FT / FA; companies 7.18 / 6.03 / 7.15 / 6.04; inventions 4.34 / 2.38 / 4.37 / 2.15. Truth does not move with frequency inside a domain. Only the cell label does. Pooled across the corpus, Cliff's delta between typical and atypical is 0.683 on true items and 0.697 on false items, both with p below 1e-24.

Five properties are enforced at build time rather than checked afterwards, and \texttt{finalize()} refuses to write a corpus that violates any of them.

\begin{itemize}
\item No statement appears twice.
\item No domain exceeds half the corpus. Cities is exactly 50.0\%, companies 26.3\%, inventions 23.7\%.
\item \texttt{edited} is drawn 50/50 inside every cell, so P(edited) is 0.500 in TT, TA, FT and FA alike.
\item No metadata field recoverable from the statement text predicts truth off diagonal after learning the association on the diagonal.
\item A domain that cannot fill all four cells is dropped whole rather than contributing to some.
\end{itemize}

The hidden state canaries pass: a probe predicting ``was this edited'' from activations reaches 0.481 [0.373, 0.580], and a probe predicting truth from tokenization features alone reaches 0.417 [0.318, 0.506]. Both intervals contain chance.

\section{Removing the frequency direction}

We next ask whether anything can be done about it. Section 3 shows the probes read familiarity. It does not show whether truth is absent from the representation or merely entangled with it, and those two possibilities call for opposite responses. If truth is absent, internal-state detection on these substrates is a dead end. If it is entangled, the confound is a preprocessing problem.

It is entangled. Throughout this section the truth probe is a logistic regression rather than the feedforward classifier of A.1, applied identically before and after erasure so that every gain is a within-probe comparison. We remove the entity-frequency direction by iterative nullspace projection \citep{ravfogel2020null}: fit a linear classifier that predicts whether an item's entity is common or rare, project the representation onto the nullspace of its weight vector, and repeat twelve times. Removing a property and re-probing is the design of amnesic probing \citep{elazar2021amnesic}; we use the iterative rather than the closed-form estimator \citep{belrose2023leace} because we want the number of removed directions to be an explicit parameter we can report and sweep, not a property of the solution. The truth probe is then trained on the diagonal exactly as before, on the projected representation.

Three properties of the procedure matter for what can be claimed from it (Figure~\ref{fig:blindness}).

The projection never sees a truth label. It is fit on frequency labels alone, and those labels are entity counts taken from a pretraining corpus rather than judgements made by a person, so producing them for a new set of statements is a lookup rather than an annotation exercise. This matters for whether the procedure is usable at all: a correction that needed truth labels on the deployment distribution would be circular, since knowing which statements are true is the problem the probe was meant to solve. This is what makes it a procedure a practitioner can run.

The projection never sees an evaluation item. It is fit on the diagonal plus half of the off-diagonal, and every number below is scored on the other half, which neither the projection nor the probe has touched. Fitting it transductively, on all items including those scored, inflates the recovery.

The frequency labels must come from decorrelated data. On the diagonal, frequency and truth are collinear by construction, so a projection fit there cannot distinguish them. The \texttt{erase-diag} column reports that control.

Averaged over all eighteen substrates, off-diagonal AUROC rises from \textbf{0.409 to 0.768}, a mean gain of \textbf{0.360}. The paired bootstrap interval on the gain excludes zero on \textbf{all eighteen models}, so this is not five seeds happening to agree. Five seeds; the table gives means over them.

The gain is that direction, not the loss of dimensions. Projecting out twelve random orthogonal directions instead leaves performance where it started, \textbf{0.408 against a baseline of 0.409}, and the per-model figures are identical to three decimals on fourteen of eighteen substrates. Nothing about discarding twelve dimensions from a several-thousand-dimensional representation recovers truth. Removing the frequency direction does.

Frequency labels drawn from the benchmark's own distribution are not enough. Fitting the projection on the diagonal alone averages 0.557, and full erasure beats it on 16 of 18 substrates. The decorrelated corpus does more than detect the confound; the frequency labels it supplies are an input to removing it.

How much is recovered tracks capability. The gain is largest where the collapse was worst, but the ceiling after erasure is not: Pythia reaches 0.752 at 12B, Qwen clears 0.9 by 1.5B, and Gemma-2-9b reaches 0.986. A practitioner can therefore predict where this will work. On substrates whose off-diagonal score after erasure remains near chance, the truth signal is not merely entangled, and no amount of preprocessing substitutes for a representation that separates truth in the first place.

Simply training on all four cells, where that is possible, is better still, averaging 0.775 and reaching 0.990 on the strongest substrates. Erasure is for the common case where the training set is fixed and the deployment distribution is not.

The fix survives on natural errors, and where the projection is fitted decides whether it is enough. Section 4 so far removes the confound from probes evaluated on falsehoods we wrote. Whether it also works where the falsehoods are a model's own errors was registered as D-026 before it was run, with both arms and both outcome sentences fixed in advance, because this is the run that could have withdrawn the claim.

In the \textbf{transfer} arm both the probe and the projection are fit on the authored corpus and applied unchanged to the 140 harvested items, which is the deployment case this section claims to serve. Its baseline column is not the same quantity as A.8's harvested off-diagonal range of 0.049 to 0.447, and the two should not be read against each other: A.8 trains the feedforward probe of A.1 on the harvested corpus's own diagonal, while here a logistic regression trained on the authored diagonal is carried across to harvested items it has never seen, with the scaler also fit on authored data because that is all a practitioner would hold at deployment. Transferring across corpora costs accuracy before any projection is applied, which is why this column runs lower, from \textbf{0.017 to 0.322}. Off-diagonal AUROC rises from \textbf{0.102 to 0.441}, a mean gain of \textbf{0.340}, and the paired interval excludes zero on all eighteen models. In the \textbf{native} arm the projection is fit on harvested frequency labels with the off-diagonal split in half and scored on the held-out half. It rises from \textbf{0.151 to 0.744}, a gain of \textbf{0.593}, again excluding zero on all eighteen.

A gain is not a rescue, and the difference between the arms is the practical result. After transfer erasure the probe is still below chance on \textbf{13 of 18} models; after native erasure it clears chance on \textbf{all 18}. We state the comparison narrowly, because the two arms differ in more than one thing: the native arm re-estimates the directions on harvested activations as well as taking its labels from there, so this is not an isolated claim about label provenance. What the contrast supports is the weaker and more useful statement. Carrying a projection over from the training distribution improves a probe without making it usable; refitting it on the deployment distribution makes it usable. Refitting needs statements from that distribution and an entity-frequency count for each, and no truth labels at all, which is why it is something a practitioner can actually do.

The registered prediction was partly wrong. We predicted the native arm would give a \emph{smaller} gain than the authored 0.330, reasoning that the harvested corpus is not frequency-crossed by construction. It is crossed, at 35 items per cell, and the native gain came in larger at 0.593. The transfer prediction, a smaller gain than authored, held.

What erasure costs on the benchmark's own number. In-distribution AUROC on the held-out diagonal falls from \textbf{0.968 to 0.718} when the frequency direction is removed, so the benchmark does see the operation, and what it sees is a loss. The mean describes no individual substrate. On Gemma-2-9b the reported number barely moves. On four Pythia substrates it falls \textbf{below chance} --- 0.257, 0.365, 0.385 and 0.449, from in-distribution scores of 0.845 to 0.979 before erasure. Where the off-diagonal score was near zero, the number the field reports was close to entirely frequency: remove that direction and almost nothing is left to separate true from false, even on the diagonal the probe was trained on.

The inversion on those four is not something we can explain and we report it as observed. One candidate is that twelve directions fit partly on diagonal items take the weak residual truth signal with them where that signal was small to begin with, which would predict the effect concentrates exactly where it does. We have not tested it, and the k sweep below does not settle it.

\textbf{How many directions.} We fixed k = 12 before scoring any held-out half, and report the sweep the choice invites rather than leaving it asserted.

\section{Further results}

Three further experiments bear on the account above without being required by it, and we summarise them here and report them in full in Appendix A.6 to A.8. Each supports the account above and none is required for it, so they are summarised here.

Training composition governs the collapse (A.6). A probe trained on Azaria and Mitchell's released dataset rather than our diagonal reaches off-diagonal AUROC of 0.697 to 0.981 with no collapse, so the shortcut is a property of how a corpus is built rather than of probing itself. That dataset still teaches it: the frequency read there is 0.574 to 0.690, every interval excluding chance, and on the true items of our natural-error corpus pythia-6.9b reaches 0.940 [0.879, 0.988]. Alignment between truth and familiarity is a spectrum, and the reported score is roughly constant across it.

The benchmark cannot select a layer either (A.7). In-distribution AUROC saturates at 1.0000 at two or more layers on eight of eighteen models, while off-diagonal performance across the layers the benchmark cannot separate spans up to 0.860. The benchmark returns one number for layers that differ enormously in what they are worth, and published selection rules do not rescue it: the earliest-informative-layer criterion forfeits 0.337 against the best layer available.

\section{Practical implications}

A detector built this way, deployed on a model where truth is not linearly available, does not fail randomly. It fails as a function of how common the subject is, flagging true statements about less common entities and passing fluent falsehoods about famous ones --- the precise inversion of what a verification tool is for, and worst on the long-tail knowledge where verification matters most.

The practical reading is narrower than a warning about probes. A reported score summarises a probe on the distribution it was fitted to, and on these corpora that distribution aligns truth with familiarity, so the score inherits the alignment along with the signal. Whether that matters depends on how far the deployment distribution sits from the training one, which is measurable in advance and rarely measured.

\section{Limitations}

The statements we test are short factual claims about cities, companies and inventions, each built from a single template per domain, so we cannot say from this evidence how the picture changes for multi-clause claims or for text a model generates freely. At 608 items the per-cell intervals are wide, and we lean throughout on the direction an effect takes across eighteen models rather than on the size of any one cell. A further complication is that all of our true statements can be found verbatim in the public Azaria and Mitchell files, so attestedness is partly entangled with the frequency axis we care about; A.4 works through what that can and cannot account for, and the bias it introduces runs against the effect we report rather than towards it. The transfer arm rests on four models rather than eighteen, and nothing in our data settles whether the knee moves for training sets larger than 1,458 items.

\section{Related work}

Probing classifiers have a long methodological literature, and two of its results bear directly on this paper. \citet{alain2017probes} introduced linear probes on intermediate representations; \citet{belinkov2022probing} surveys what they can and cannot establish. \citet{hewitt2019control} is the closest methodological ancestor: control tasks ask whether a probe reads the property it claims or a shortcut available in the representation, which is our question with typicality as the shortcut and a corpus rather than a random-label control as the instrument.

The design also has a direct ancestor outside probing. \citet{mccoy2019hans} train on data where a heuristic and the label agree, then test where they disagree, and find models collapse --- the same move as our diagonal and off-diagonal, in natural language inference. \citet{gururangan2018artifacts} show the artifacts that make such collapses possible are already present in widely used corpora, and \citep{geirhos2020shortcut} give the general account of shortcut learning our result is an instance of. For hallucination detection as a task, \citep{ji2023hallucination} survey the setting.

That internal-state probes read something other than truth has been observed before. \citet{levinstein2023still} show they fail on negation and generalise poorly. \citet{farquhar2023challenges} show unsupervised probes latch onto salient non-knowledge features. \citet{cheang2026really} give a mechanistic account on which probes track parametric knowledge recall, which our §A.7.3 dissociation supports directly. \citet{seo2026quantifying} decompose benchmark performance by question-side information and find heavy shortcut exploitation.

Our frequency axis descends from work on entity popularity and long-tail factual accuracy. \citet{mallen2023trust} show model accuracy falls with subject popularity and that retrieval helps precisely there; \citet{kandpal2023large} show factual accuracy tracks pretraining occurrence count. Those results establish that frequency governs what a model knows. We show it also governs what a probe reports about what the model knows, which is a different claim about a different instrument.

\citet{hussain2026parallax} are closest in spirit. They show that four of six popular hallucination benchmarks embed the reference answer in the prompt, so a text-similarity baseline with no access to internals reaches 0.98 AUROC on HaluEval. Their artifact is lexical and lives in existing corpora; ours is distributional and required building a corpus to expose. Our §5 result is the distributional sibling of theirs: a trivial baseline matches or beats the field's methods, so the benchmark is substantially measuring construction.

We differ from all of these in what we measure. Those results establish that a confound exists. We quantify how much of a reported number it accounts for, on a corpus verified to decorrelate the axis and gated against five construction failures we committed ourselves, and we show what the reported number does and does not carry: it ranks endorsed probes at 0.453, and half of those are below chance.

\subsection{Recoverability}

Recoverability is the standardized truth coefficient from a regression of out-of-fold all-cell probe scores on truth, with entity frequency, tokenization fragmentation and edit provenance partialled out. Probe scores are five-fold cross-fitted, so no item is scored by a probe that saw it.

The canonical estimator is \textbf{cross-fitted across items} (D-024): recoverability is estimated on one half of the corpus, stratified by cell, and the off-diagonal probe evaluated on the other, then swapped and averaged, so the two quantities share no item. Same-sample and cross-fitted estimates correlate at Spearman 0.9856 but differ by up to 0.165 on individual models, which is why the estimator is named rather than assumed.

This works against the effect we report: true statements sitting in the false cell make a truth-reading probe look worse, not better.

\subsection{The benchmark cannot select a layer either}

Every result above reads one layer per model. The literature disagrees about which layer carries truth, so we swept all of them, across eighteen models and four probe architectures (Figure~\ref{fig:layers}).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/fig07_layers.png}
\caption{Logistic regression on Gemma-2-9b-it. The lower panel shows three further saturating models on a normalised depth axis; the full set of eight is listed below. Both series are AUROC.}
\label{fig:layers}
\end{figure}

It can say almost nothing, and the reason is saturation. We call a model saturated when gradient-trained logistic regression reaches in-distribution AUROC of exactly 1.0000 at two or more layers, which happens on eight of the eighteen: Gemma-2-9b (28 such layers), Gemma-2-9b-it (25), Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (24), Llama-3.1-8B (21), Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (17), gemma-2-2b (15), Qwen2.5-7B (13) and Qwen2.5-3B (10). Widening to layers within 0.01 of the maximum adds more on every one of them. Across the layers it scores indistinguishably, off-diagonal AUROC ranges from 0.015 to 0.875. Mass-mean probing and TTPD mostly have a unique best layer with zero spread, because their in-distribution scores top out below 1.0 and never saturate. Discrimination is weakest at the top of the in-distribution range.

Published selection rules do not rescue this. Averaged over 72 probe-model pairs, the ``earliest informative layer'' criterion lands on layers averaging \textbf{0.333 off-diagonal and forfeits 0.337} against the best layer available. Fixed fractional depths in the upper third, as used by \citep{hussain2026parallax}, average 0.507 to 0.533. Our own mid-depth choice averages 0.503, marginally the worst of the fixed rules, and we report that rather than presenting our layer as neutral. Every headline number in §3 uses that mid-depth layer.

Discordance counts items where the probe crosses 0.5 upward while behaviour crosses downward, which is scale-free where the two means are not. \subsubsection{A sampling-based detector shows no frequency penalty}

Semantic entropy \citep{farquhar2024semantic} never looks at the residual stream. On three instruction-tuned models --- Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Gemma-2-9b-it, 180 subjects each --- it detects each model's own errors at 0.776, 0.822 and 0.758. Restricted to statements each model got right, the exact contrast that exposes the probes, it separates rare true from common true at \textbf{0.514 [0.465, 0.565], 0.500 [0.440, 0.561] and 0.509 [0.474, 0.546]}, chance on all three. So the frequency confound is a property of linear readouts of hidden states, not of the model's own uncertainty.

\subsection{A second corpus, with falsehoods we did not write}

Everything above rests on statements we constructed, and two of our five corpus builds produced the effect we were looking for as an artifact of their construction. This section repeats the central measurement on falsehoods that came from a model rather than from us.

The second corpus's false statements are mistakes a model actually made. We asked Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, under greedy decoding, which country each of four thousand cities is in, and kept the answers that were wrong. That model is not among the eighteen we evaluate, though its base model is. To decide what counts as wrong we used a multi-valued Wikidata gazetteer rather than the single-country knowledge base behind our other corpus: measured directly, \textbf{46\% of the errors that base identified were statements that are actually true}. The result is 140 statements, 35 per cell.

The pattern reported in the body holds on this corpus as well. Every model scores 0.875 to 1.000 on the benchmark. Off-diagonal, all eighteen fall between \textbf{0.049 and 0.447}. Per-cell mean probe scores across the eighteen are 0.904 true-common, 0.254 true-rare, \textbf{0.661 false-common}, 0.087 false-rare: probes rate a model's errors above true statements about rare entities.

Two internals-free methods clear the bar all eighteen probes miss: semantic entropy at 0.758 to 0.822, and reference perplexity at 0.770. Probes trained on the field's own data reach 0.309 to 0.767 on it (§A.6.1), and none of them beats the perplexity baseline. Natural error is detectable by the training-free baselines and not by the probes.

The mechanism does not carry over. Recoverability and off-diagonal AUROC correlate at 0.490 [-0.077, 0.754], against 0.821 on the authored corpus, and model comparison no longer prefers the hinge.

One sibling check, since the generator's base model is among the eighteen. Qwen2.5-1.5B is the base of Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, which produced these errors, so a probe on it might be expected to treat them differently. It does not: it rates the false-common cell at 0.712 against 0.658 for the other seventeen, sixth of eighteen, sitting inside the pack rather than apart from it.

Three reasons to treat this corpus as provisional. Its false-common cell is capped at 35 items because models rarely err about famous places, which is itself a fact about natural errors. Its tokenization canary reads 0.636 [0.409, 0.866], passing only because the interval is wide at this size. And roughly three thousand candidate items were dropped for want of frequency counts, which is what holds it at 140.

\subsection{Why the collapse happens, and in what shape}

The last column of the results table is recoverability: how well truth can be linearly decoded from the same hidden states when the probe is trained on decorrelated data instead of the diagonal, as a partial regression coefficient with typicality, fragmentation and edit provenance held out (Appendix A.3). It is a property of the representation, not of the probe.

Recoverability and off-diagonal AUROC correlate at r = 0.821 [0.706, 0.92] when both are estimated on the same 608 items. That sharing inflates nothing: estimating them on disjoint halves raises the correlation to \textbf{0.910}, so shared sampling noise had been attenuating the relation rather than manufacturing it. The cross-fitted estimate is canonical (D-024).

The relation is not a line. Comparing three shapes by AIC gives linear -60.65, step -74.88, and hinge -90.12:

\begin{quote}
off\_diagonal = 0.164 [0.126, 0.204] + 4.81 [1.88, 7.65] x max(0, recoverability - 0.765 [0.57, 0.80])
\end{quote}

That equation is the \textbf{same-sample} fit, kept because the figure and the AIC comparison above are computed from it. Under the canonical cross-fitted estimator (D-024) the same fit puts the knee at \textbf{0.659}, which is the value the ladder below reports at its full rung. Both are quoted wherever the knee is used; the paper's central parameter therefore has one value per estimator and never a third (Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}).

Below the knee, nine models spanning recoverability from -0.072 to 0.585 all sit between 0.079 and 0.253 off-diagonal. Above it, off-diagonal AUROC climbs steeply. The substitution is total until a threshold is crossed.

We settled the functional form with new data rather than by fit quality. At an earlier stage, with thirteen models, a step function fit best and its threshold fell inside a region of recoverability where we had no models at all. Rather than adopt the better-fitting shape, we ran five models chosen to land in that gap; Qwen2.5-1.5B came back interpolating rather than jumping, which refuted the step.

What the knee is a property of is undetermined, and we registered the test before running it. If the knee moves with training size, the hinge describes a probe regime rather than a representation. \subsection{Scale is not the variable}

Within the Pythia family, going from 70m to 12b does not clean the probe up. Off-diagonal AUROC across that whole ladder stays between 0.079 and 0.253 while recoverability climbs from -0.026 to 0.585. Parameter count is not what determines whether a probe reads truth. What moves the number is whether the model has a linearly accessible truth representation for the probe to find.

\subsection{Four construction artifacts, and the gates that catch them}

Four earlier corpora produced clean, large, cross-model effects that we believed at the time. Each was an artifact of our own construction, and each is worth stating because the checks that catch them are cheap and are now released.

The first ranked frequency terciles across pooled domains. Because domains had different frequency profiles, the diagonal ended up 32\% non-cities among true items and 0\% among false ones, while the off diagonal reversed that exactly. ``Non-city implies true'' was a perfect shortcut in training that inverted at test, so a probe reading nothing but topic scored below chance off diagonal. We reported that as a typicality confound for three weeks. Holding domain fixed removed the whole effect.

The second was worse and simpler. The frequency cache recorded failed API calls as \texttt{count = 0}. Because the endpoint rate limits, 2627 of 2884 entities, or 91\%, were stored as having zero occurrences. Amman's real count is 1,183,125. Paris is 58,070,155. Both were zero. The typicality axis was not entity frequency at all. It was whether the network call happened to succeed, which correlated with domain, which produced the first artifact. One bug, both failures. Its reach was larger than the corpus: a downstream analysis (§A.7.3) silently lost an entire stratum to the same defect, unnoticed for eleven months, and found by a provenance gate on its first run.

The third passed both of those gates and still carried a defect. Seventy of its 696 rows were exact duplicate statements, concentrated almost entirely in the two true cells, because a truth-preserving swap could land on a string already present as an original. Per-cell counts were equal in rows and unequal in distinct statements, 141 / 139 / 174 / 172.

The fourth is the subtlest and we found it only by running an invariant test that had been in the repository since July 2026. The generator left the FA cell edit-heavy, 0.578 against roughly 0.42 elsewhere. Since the training diagonal is TT plus FA, ``edited'' predicted false in training and predicted nothing off diagonal. That is the same signature as the confound under study. Using the \texttt{edited} flag itself as the predictor, which bounds what any probe reading provenance perfectly could extract, it was worth a gap of +0.075 AUROC before a probe read a single hidden state.

The corpus described above is the fifth attempt. On it, that same bound is \textbf{+0.0000}: a perfect edit shortcut scores 0.500 on the diagonal and 0.500 off it.

\subsection{Gates for experiments, not just for corpora}

Every experiment in this paper ran outside any equivalent check, and several failed the way corpora did. The record, because it is the argument for the tooling:

1. \textbf{A contaminated experiment.} The §A.6 transfer test was first run without excluding our evaluation items from its training set, reported near-perfect transfer, and was written into a draft as a headline correction. What caught it was a reader noticing that a row count did not match the dataset it was attributed to. 2. \textbf{A silently lost measure.} A rerun stopped reporting a behaviour-versus-probe comparison for a week. A column-parity gate, diffing the leaf measures of every superseding artifact against the run it replaces, found it on its first execution, and the cause turned out to be the frequency-cache defect of §A.11 propagating eleven months downstream. 3. \textbf{A conclusion the registration refused.} The knee ladder came back monotone on all five rungs, with a monotone slope beside it, and a shift of 0.217 against a bound of 0.230 fixed in advance. Chosen afterwards, that threshold would have licensed a migration claim. 4. \textbf{A mechanism withdrawn by simulation.} We attributed the ladder's drift to regression dilution. A two-target test, since attenuation predicts both a leftward knee and a flatter slope, reproduced the knee at five of five rungs and the slope at two of five. The claim was withdrawn rather than softened. 5. \textbf{A shared prediction failure.} The follow-up correlated-noise analysis was expected by both parties to be unnecessary. It was necessary, and it changed the reading of the residual.

The released tooling therefore carries two registers. \textbf{Corpus gates}, the five in §2, which run inside \texttt{finalize()}. And \textbf{experiment gates}: entity-level exclusion between any training and evaluation set, a provenance manifest recording source URL, retrieval date, sha256 and expected row count for every file under \texttt{data/}, and a manuscript checker that lints the prose, the figure-generation source, and every three-decimal number in the draft against the artifact directory. Explained historical findings live in a ledger the checker prints but does not fail on, because a gate that fails on every run stops being read.
